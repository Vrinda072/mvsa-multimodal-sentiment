What is multimodal sentiment analysis?

Sentiment analysis assigns a feeling to a piece of content: negative, neutral or positive. A social media post usually has two parts, a picture and a caption, and each can carry its own feeling. A multimodal model reads both parts and combines what it finds. This matters most when the parts disagree: a cheerful picture under a gloomy caption can be sarcasm, and the page flags it when it happens.

The model on this page was trained on MVSA-Single, a set of Twitter posts where people labelled the text and the image separately. It is built from a few simple parts:

  1. An encoder turns a picture or a caption into a list of numbers called an embedding. This model uses CLIP ViT-B/32, a network trained to match pictures with their captions. It is kept frozen: none of its weights are changed here.
  2. A classifier turns an embedding into three probabilities, one per class. Each classifier here is a logistic regression.
  3. Fusion combines the classifiers. This model uses late fusion: each classifier decides on its own and only the probabilities are averaged.
  4. A class score is a probability between 0 and 1. The three scores always sum to 1, and the highest one is the prediction.

What does each step of the model do?

Click a step in the diagram above while you read. Each one opens a worked example with this post's own numbers.

Input

The picture and the caption stay separate from start to finish. The picture is resized and centre-cropped to 224 × 224 pixels with three colour channels, the size CLIP was trained on. The caption is cleaned the way the training tweets were: links are removed, @mentions are replaced, hashtags are split into words and emoji are written as words. Words written inside a picture are not read out; only the picture encoder sees them.

CLIP encoders

CLIP's vision transformer turns the picture into 512 numbers, and its text transformer does the same for the caption. In the diagram each embedding is drawn as a 32 × 16 grid. A cell is red when that value is below the training average and blue when it is above. Because CLIP learned to place a picture near its caption, the two embeddings live in comparable spaces, which the agreement section below uses.

Word features

The caption is also counted word by word. TF-IDF scores each word and word pair by how often it appears in this caption and how rare it is across all training captions, using a 5,000-term vocabulary. This gives the model a direct view of sentiment words such as "happy", which a general-purpose embedding can blur.

Logistic regression

Three classifiers work side by side, one per encoder output. Each multiplies every input value by a learned weight, adds the products, and gets one score per class. Strong regularisation (C = 0.001) keeps every weight on the CLIP embeddings small, so no single dimension decides.

Figure 2. One model's three class scores build up as each value × weight is added: the eight strongest dimensions first, then the other 504 together, then the constant.
Softmax
pk = exp(zk) / (exp(zneg) + exp(zneu) + exp(zpos))

Softmax turns the three scores z into probabilities that sum to 1. A class with a much higher score than the others takes nearly all the probability; three similar scores give three similar probabilities.

Figure 3. Softmax on that model's scores: each score is exponentiated, then divided by the sum of the three.

Late fusion

The three classifiers vote with their probabilities, and each vote counts equally. The mean of the three is the fused reading. Late fusion keeps the picture and the caption as separate judgements, so a strong picture cannot hide a contradicting caption inside one shared vector.

Figure 4. Late fusion: the three models' probabilities, one model at a time, and then their mean.
Rescaling neutral

Before the final answer, the neutral probability is multiplied by 0.75 and the three are rescaled to sum to 1. The factor was chosen by cross-validation on the training and validation posts. It trades some neutral recall for fewer false neutrals, which raised both accuracy and macro-F1.

Figure 5. Rescaling: the neutral probability is multiplied by 0.75, then all three are divided by their new sum.

Do the caption and the picture agree?

A sarcastic or mixed post shows up as disagreement between what the caption says and what the picture shows. CLIP places a caption close to its picture when the caption describes it, so their cosine similarity measures fit.

Two further models read each part alone: a caption-only model trained on the text labels and a picture-only model trained on the image labels. Jensen–Shannon divergence compares their readings, from 0 bits when they match to 1 bit when they share nothing. Entropy measures how undecided a reading is, from 0 bits when one class takes everything to 1.585 bits when all three are equal.

Which words moved the word model?

The word model is linear, so its decision splits exactly into one contribution per term: the term's weight in the caption times what the model learned for it. Each bar is the push towards positive minus the push towards negative.

How reliable is the prediction?

The model was scored once on 447 held-out test posts that played no part in training or in choosing settings. A post is neutral only when both its text and its image are neutral; posts whose text and image point in opposite directions have no single label and are left out. The main reported model scores 0.696 accuracy and 0.603 macro-F1. This page runs the follow-up model chosen by cross-validation, which scores 0.761 and 0.643.

How is this page implemented?

The embeddings come from a frozen CLIP ViT-B/32. The classifiers are scikit-learn logistic regressions, saved as plain weight arrays. The page is a single HTML file with no libraries, served by a small Python standard-library server that runs the model and returns the numbers drawn here.
